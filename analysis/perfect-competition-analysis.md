# Perfect Competition: Analysis

## 1. Why tomatoes stop at about 10 beds

Tomato profit increases steadily through Bed 10, reaching a maximum of $26,172.77 (H14), before declining at Bed 11 to $25,582.06 (H15). This decline is driven by compounding labor costs, which begin to outweigh the additional revenue generated by expanding production.  At Bed 10, labor costs total $53,027.23 (D14), and total costs are $61,827.23 (F14). At Bed 11, labor costs increase to $61,537.94 (D15), an increase of $8,510.71, or approximately 16.1%. Total costs rise to $71,217.94 (F15), an increase of $9,390.71. Labor accounts for approximately 86.4% of total costs at Bed 11, making it the primary driver of the cost increase.

Although expanding from 10 to 11 beds increases production, the additional costs reduce overall profit by $590.71. Therefore, planting 10 beds is the most economically advantageous option among the production levels evaluated. Expanding beyond 10 beds would be financially inefficient under the current cost structure because the additional production reduces, rather than increases, total profit.

## 2. Which constraints bind, and what relaxing one is worth

The maximum bed constraints are binding, limiting carrot production to 20 beds and mesclun production to 30 beds. Since tomato production yields maximum profit at 10 beds, four beds remain unused under the current constraints. Rather than leaving this capacity idle, the farm could improve overall profitability by relaxing the maximum bed constraint for carrots.

The shadow price for carrots at the 21-bed level is $352.50 (B19), compared with $246.48 for mesclun (B20). This indicates that, within the allowable range of the sensitivity analysis, expanding the carrot production constraint by one bed would increase the objective value by $352.50, while expanding the mesclun constraint by one bed would increase it by $246.48. Therefore, carrots offer a greater marginal return on additional bed capacity.

I recommend the farm to consider relaxing the maximum bed constraint for carrots first, allowing the unused capacity to be allocated to a more profitable crop. This approach could increase total farm profit without expanding the overall growing area, provided the shadow prices remain valid within the sensitivity analysis's allowable ranges.

## 3. The tomato marginal-cost dip

Tomato marginal cost increases steadily through Bed 5, reaching $7,660.86 (I9), before dropping to $4,906.28 at Bed 6 (I10). This decrease occurs because the farmer’s 720 available labor hours are initially valued at $34.72 per hour. Beginning at Bed 6, the model incorporates temporary labor at a lower wage of $17.36 per hour, reducing the marginal cost of additional production.  After Bed 6, marginal cost begins to rise again as production expands and labor requirements increase. At Bed 9, marginal cost reaches $7,243.78 (I13), which remains below the $7,660.86 recorded at Bed 5. However, by Bed 10, marginal cost rises to $8,248.59 (I14), exceeding the cost at Bed 5 as the labor penalty catches up with the additional production.

These fluctuations illustrate how the availability of lower-cost temporary labor initially reduces marginal cost, while increasing labor requirements eventually drive costs upward. This supports the conclusion that expanding tomato production beyond the optimal level may not be economically justified.
