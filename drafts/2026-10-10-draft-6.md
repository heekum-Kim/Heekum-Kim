Challenge
Community care homes in Hawaiʻi face a significant financial challenge: low net profits despite the responsibility of providing resident care around the clock. For many care home owners, the income generated by their businesses is insufficient to make operating a care home their sole source of livelihood. Meanwhile, Hawaiʻi’s population aged 85 and older is projected to more than double over the next two decades. Where will these residents go when they need affordable long-term care? This issue is personal to me. From my own experience, finding a quality care home for my late mother-in-law was extremely difficult. What began as a personal challenge has become a growing concern among my friends and relatives as more families face the challenge of finding appropriate and affordable care for their aging loved ones.

Economic Concepts
Estimated net profit of approximately $35,000 to $60,000 for a 2-bed foster care home vs. an RN's hospital pay of $136,320 ($65.54/hour) (State of Hawaii Department of Labor and Industrial Relations [DLIR], 2025) (opportunity cost; incentives) which discourages registered nurses from entering the field of care home business. I have registered nurse friends running a care home as a side business because the income it generates does not measure up to RN's hospital pay. It simply does not make economic sense. Care homes keep people out of costlier nursing homes (positive externality; public savings) however, when care home owners must rely on additional employment to supplement their business income and obtain employee benefits, their time and availability to oversee resident care may be limited. This raises an important question: Who will provide consistent oversight and ensure residents receive quality care if owners cannot earn a sustainable living from operating their homes? GET charged on gross receipts, not profit (Tax burden on a low-margin business) According to care home analysis it can be from $3855 (10.9% of net profit of $35,411, personal estimation) to $5005 (8.4% of net profit of $59,828, personal estimation) depending on level of care and payment mix of Medicaid and private pay. These amounts represent a substantial financial burden for small care home operators whose businesses provide essential community service.

Analysis
Figure 1 illustrates the growing gap between Hawaiʻi’s aging population and its existing community care home capacity. By 2030, slightly more than three years from the time of this analysis, the number of residents aged 85 and older is projected to reach 51,677 (Hawaiʻi Department of Business, Economic Development and Tourism [DBEDT], 2024). Based on the current count of 3,281 certified care home beds, this represents approximately 16 residents aged 85 and older for every available bed (Hawaiʻi Statewide GIS Program, 2026). If care home capacity does not expand to meet future needs, more families may have to seek placement in nursing homes, which are significantly more expensive. Annual nursing home costs are estimated at $101,000–$123,000 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [CMS], 2024), compared with approximately $45,100 annually for a Level 2 community care home resident receiving Medicaid-funded services (Hawaiʻi Department of Human Services [DHS], 2025). This cost difference highlights the potential financial value of sustaining community-based care capacity.
 
Recommendation
I recommend explicitly extending the GET exemption to community care foster family homes, Adult Residential Care Homes (ARCHs), and Expanded Adult Residential Care Homes (E-ARCHs). For small care home operators, eliminating a tax burden that can represent approximately 8.4%–10.9% of annual net profit could provide meaningful financial relief and help improve the sustainability of these businesses. Although the exemption alone may not resolve the financial challenges facing care home operators, it would be a meaningful first step. Additional measures, such as reviewing and potentially increasing Level 2 reimbursement rates, could further improve the financial viability of community-based care. The state sets the price with “inclusive of all applicable taxes” (DHS, 2025) and care home cannot pass GET on to anyone but pay themselves. Act 47 added the exemption as HRS §237-24.3(12), effective January 1, 2026 (Hawaii Department of Taxation, 2025). However, the guidance lists eligible providers but does not define “health care facility” or name care homes (Hawaii Department of Taxation, 2025). Therefore, I am recommending the Legislature amends §237-24.3(12) to name community care foster family homes, ARCHs, and E-ARCHs explicitly. With that said, the objection will be the lost state tax revenue which is approximately $5,850,000 which is upper estimate due to not every home is full or all-Medicaid (1,300 homes X $4,500 which is my midpoint of $3,855–$5,005) from state database (Hawaii Statewide GIS Program, 2026). On the other hand, if Medicaid pays for a Level 2 resident going to a nursing home instead due to lack of availability of care homes, it will roughly cost $8500 to $10,200 (an approximate base rate built from the components) for a month about $101,000–$123,000 a year according to Hawaii's Medicaid State Plan Amendment HI-23-0014, effective January 1, 2024 (CMS, 2024). Compared to the care home for the same Level 2 is about $45,100 a year (DHS, 2025) which costs roughly $56,000–$78,000 less per year than in a nursing home. Using the more conservative savings estimate of $56,000 per resident, dividing the projected $5.85 million in annual tax revenue loss by $56,000 yields approximately 104 residents. In other words, the gross difference in annual care costs for approximately 104 residents remaining in community care homes rather than requiring nursing home placement would equal the estimated tax revenue loss. One thing to consider is though it is widely known that Medicaid costs are shared between federal and state governments so the state needs more than 104 residents to remain at care home to break even.

Conclusion
Hawaiʻi must plan for the growing needs of its aging population. Access to affordable long-term care is not simply a healthcare issue; it is also an economic and community concern that affects families across the state. Community care homes provide an essential service, yet their financial constraints may discourage qualified healthcare professionals from entering or remaining in the field. Explicitly extending the GET exemption to community care foster family homes, ARCHs, and E-ARCHs would be a practical starting point toward improving the sustainability of these businesses. By reducing unnecessary financial pressure on care home operators, Hawaiʻi can take a step toward preserving community-based care capacity, supporting those who provide care, and helping families find affordable options for their aging loved ones.
